Does an Intimate Wedding Actually Suit You?

By Louise7 min readIntimate Weddings

When people say they want an intimate wedding, they usually mean a few different things. Sometimes they mean small guest list. Sometimes they mean low-key. Sometimes they mean a celebration with no fuss, no formality, no bits they dread. And sometimes they just mean they cannot imagine standing up in front of two hundred people and enjoying it.

All of those are valid reasons to consider going small. But an intimate wedding is not automatically easier, cheaper or simpler than a larger one. It is a different thing. And before you commit to it, it is worth being honest about whether it actually suits you.

What does intimate actually mean?

There is no official definition. When wedding professionals talk about intimate weddings, they usually mean somewhere between 10 and 50 guests. Some would stretch to 70. Very small ceremonies with just witnesses are sometimes called elopements, though the term gets used loosely.

What matters more than the number is the intention behind it. An intimate wedding is one where the guest list has been built around closeness, not obligation. Where every person in the room knows why they are there. Where the celebration reflects what you two actually care about rather than what a wedding is supposed to look like.

Signs it might genuinely suit you

You are both introverts, or one of you is

This one is obvious, but it is worth naming. If the idea of spending six or eight hours as the centre of attention in a room full of people sounds exhausting, a small wedding will feel like relief. You will still be the focus, but in a room of 25 people rather than 150, it is a fundamentally different feeling.

Your families are small, dispersed or complicated

Weddings built around large families often end up shaped by those families whether you intend it or not. If yours is small, you live far from each other, or there are complexities that make a traditional large reception hard to navigate, an intimate wedding removes a lot of the pressure. You can build the day around the people who will genuinely make it better.

You care more about the experience than the occasion

Some people love the occasion of a wedding. The grandeur, the ceremony, the spectacle of it. That is completely legitimate. But if what you care about is the experience, the conversation, the food you actually want to eat, the moments you will actually remember, a smaller wedding makes all of that easier to get right.

You want to spend on quality rather than quantity

A smaller guest list often means you can redirect budget into things that matter more to you: an extraordinary photographer, a restaurant you have always wanted to hire out, a hotel that feels genuinely special. You are not spreading a fixed budget across 150 covers. You are concentrating it on fewer people and higher quality.

"The couples who choose intimate weddings intentionally, rather than by default, almost always tell me afterwards that it felt completely right. Not like a compromise. Like the wedding they were supposed to have."

You are willing to have hard conversations with your guest list

This is the one people skip over, and it is probably the most important. A small wedding means someone who expected to be invited is not going to be. That conversation is uncomfortable. If the thought of it feels impossible, that is worth sitting with. It does not mean a small wedding is wrong for you, but it does mean you need a plan for navigating it.

Signs it might not suit you, or might be more complicated than you think

You have always pictured something bigger

Be honest with yourself about this. Not what you think you should want, but what you actually want. If you have always had a clear image of a full ceremony, a first dance, a full room, letting go of that for practical reasons might leave you with regret. If the smaller version genuinely excites you, that is a different story.

Your families have strong expectations

Family pressure is probably the most common reason couples end up with a wedding that does not feel like theirs. Intimate weddings can amplify this problem: with fewer spaces available, the question of who makes the cut becomes much more loaded. If your families have strong views about who should be there, you need to go into this with a clear strategy and a united front.

You are going small to cut costs, not because it is what you want

Budget is a completely reasonable factor in deciding the shape of your wedding. But going small purely to save money, while secretly wanting something bigger, usually produces a day that feels like a compromise. It is worth working out what you actually want first, and then figuring out the budget, rather than the other way around.

The question underneath all of this

When I work with couples thinking about an intimate wedding, I often ask them one question: if everyone was equally happy to come or not come, and there were no family expectations and no budget pressure, what would you choose?

Their answer is usually very clear. And it is usually the right answer.

If you are still working out what yours is, let us talk. That is exactly the kind of conversation I am here for.

FAQs

Is an intimate wedding less memorable than a big one?

Not in the least. Many couples say their small wedding was more memorable, precisely because they were actually present for it. When you are not managing 200 people, you notice things. The laughter during the ceremony. What your partner said in their vows. The dinner that went on for three hours because nobody wanted it to end.

How do I tell people they are not invited?

Honestly and early. Do not wait until they ask, and do not over-explain. Something like: "We're keeping it really small, just immediate family and our closest friends, so it'll be a tiny group." Most people understand. Some will be hurt regardless of how you say it. That is their response to manage, not yours.

Can we have a bigger party afterwards?

Yes, and many couples do. A smaller ceremony followed by a bigger celebration a few weeks later (sometimes called a "wedding party" or just a party) is a very workable solution. You get the intimate ceremony you actually want and you can still celebrate with the wider circle.

What is the smallest a wedding can be?

Legally in England and Wales, you need two witnesses plus the officiant. So technically, four people. Some couples do choose to get married with just witnesses present, which is sometimes called an elopement or a micro wedding. Everything beyond that is a choice.
