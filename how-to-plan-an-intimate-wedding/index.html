How to Plan an Intimate Wedding: A Step-by-Step Guide

By Louise9 min readIntimate Weddings · Planning Advice

Planning an intimate wedding sounds straightforward until you start. The guest list you thought would be easy turns into a complicated negotiation. The venue search reveals that many places have minimums built for a hundred people. The decisions that feel small turn out to have implications for other decisions you have not made yet.

This guide is a practical step-by-step, written for couples planning a genuinely small wedding in the UK, typically 10 to 50 guests. It is not a checklist. It is a sequence, because the order matters.

Step 1: Agree on what you actually want, before anyone else gets involved

This is the step most couples skip, and it is the one that causes most of the trouble later.

Before you tell your parents, before you look at venues, before you do anything else, sit down with your partner and answer two questions: What does the day need to feel like? And who needs to be there for it to feel right?

Write it down. Not a guest list yet, just the feeling. Intimate and unhurried. Outside if possible. Dinner rather than a reception. Good food, long table, everyone sitting together. Something like that. This becomes your reference point for every decision that follows.

When someone suggests adding more people or changing something fundamental, you can come back to this and ask honestly: does that fit what we said we wanted?

Step 2: Work out your guest list (properly, not aspirationally)

The guest list is the hardest part of planning a small wedding. This is where most couples run into trouble, so it is worth being methodical.

Start with your non-negotiables

Who absolutely has to be there? Not "would like to be there." Has to be. Write those names first. For most couples, this is a small number, usually somewhere between 5 and 20 people.

Build outward carefully

From your non-negotiables, ask: who would genuinely make the day better? Not who would be upset to miss it, but who would add something. This distinction matters. Obligation and love are both real, but they produce different guest lists.

Set a firm number

Decide on a maximum before you start telling people about the wedding. Once a number is set, it is much easier to hold. "We're keeping it to 30 people" is a complete sentence. It does not require justification.

"The couples who struggle most with the guest list are the ones who kept it fluid for too long. Set a number early, and hold it. It becomes your best friend."

Step 3: Choose the right kind of venue

Not all venues work well for small weddings. A room designed for 120 people, filled with 25, feels wrong. The scale is off. The energy disperses. Finding a venue that actually suits your numbers is essential.

What to look for

  • Rooms or spaces designed for smaller numbers (private dining rooms, converted barns, intimate ceremony spaces)
  • No minimum spend that requires you to over-cater or invite people you do not want
  • A setting that reflects what you want the day to feel like
  • Flexibility on format (can you do a long dinner rather than a formal reception?)

Good options for intimate weddings in the UK

  • Restaurant private dining rooms (many can accommodate 20 to 60 people and do not have the overheads of dedicated wedding venues)
  • Small country houses and manor houses with limited capacity
  • Boutique hotels with intimate event spaces
  • Register offices followed by a separate reception venue
  • Private members clubs
  • Destination venues abroad, particularly in Europe (Greece, Italy, France)

Step 4: Book the legal ceremony

In England and Wales, all legally recognised ceremonies require advance notice at a register office, regardless of whether you then get married at a register office, an approved venue, or through a religious ceremony.

If you are getting married at a register office, book early. Popular boroughs like Marylebone and Islington in London book up 12 months or more ahead. If you are having a humanist or celebrant-led ceremony at a private venue, your celebrant does not conduct the legal marriage: you will need a separate legal ceremony at a register office, or a registrar to attend your venue.

Step 5: Choose your suppliers in this order

Supplier availability drives your date as much as venue availability. The order matters.

  1. Venue (this usually locks your date)
  2. Photographer (good wedding photographers, particularly those who specialise in small weddings, book up quickly)
  3. Celebrant or registrar (if you are having a ceremony on site)
  4. Catering (if the venue does not handle this)
  5. Everything else (flowers, music, accommodation)

For a small wedding, you may not need all of these. Many couples keep it very simple: venue, photographer, flowers, food. That is enough.

Step 6: Think about the day's shape

Small weddings work best when the day has a shape that suits the scale. You are not running a programme for 150 people. You have 25. Things can be more relaxed, more flexible, more human.

Some structures that work well:

  • Short ceremony, drinks, long relaxed lunch, afternoon, home
  • Ceremony, dinner, overnight stay (this works particularly well at small country houses and destination venues)
  • Afternoon ceremony, evening dinner, dancing for the core group

Resist the temptation to programme every minute. With a small guest list, you can trust the room. Good people, good food, good wine. The day takes care of itself.

Step 7: Communicate clearly with your guests

Small weddings often need more communication with guests, not less, because individual dynamics matter more. A few things worth doing:

  • Tell people early that the wedding is small, so nobody is surprised
  • Give clear information about the day's format (dress code, timings, overnight logistics)
  • If you have a mix of family and friends who do not know each other, consider how you can help people connect before and during the day

FAQs

How long does it take to plan an intimate wedding?

Most couples planning a small wedding in the UK take 6 to 18 months. If you are flexible on dates and location, 6 months is feasible. If you have a specific venue or photographer in mind, 12 months or more is safer. Destination weddings often need 12 to 18 months due to travel logistics and venue availability.

Do I need a wedding planner for a small wedding?

Not necessarily, but it depends on the complexity of what you are planning. A simple UK wedding with a small guest list and a venue that handles most logistics can often be managed without one. If you are planning a destination wedding, a multi-part day, or if you simply want someone to hold the logistics so you can enjoy the process, a planner or coordinator can be worth it.

What is the biggest mistake couples make when planning an intimate wedding?

Not deciding on a guest list number early enough, and then letting it drift upward. Once you tell one person who was not on the original list, you create a precedent. Set a firm number early, hold it, and do not negotiate with yourself.

Can we have a ceremony and a party at different times?

Yes. Some couples have a small ceremony with immediate family and closest friends, then a larger party a few weeks or months later for a wider circle. This separates the wedding itself from the obligation of including everyone, and can work very well.
